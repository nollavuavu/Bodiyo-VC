## 1. Mechanical tension, fiber recruitment and "effective reps"

**One-line answer:** Muscle grows mainly in response to high tension on its fibers, and sets that end close to failure deliver that tension with light or heavy weights.

**Evidence grade:** Moderate — tension as the main driver and similar growth from light and heavy sets taken to failure are well supported, but the "effective reps" idea is a theoretical model that has not been tested directly.

**(a) App justification:** Muscles grow mainly in response to high mechanical tension on their fibers. As a set gets hard, your body recruits its largest, most growth-prone motor units, and the bar slows down. According to a leading model, the slow, hard reps near failure are when those fibers work hardest. That's why light and heavy sets built similar muscle in trials where both were taken to failure. In several velocity-based trials, letting the bar slow down more within each set (stopping closer to failure) built more muscle, although those sets also included more reps; when total reps were roughly matched, results were mixed: two small trials found no difference and one found slightly more thigh growth with more slowing.

**(b) Recommendation:** Make every working set end hard: the last few reps should slow down noticeably even though you're pushing as hard as you can.

**(d) Evidence strength:** Moderate. Mechanical tension as the main stimulus is well supported by mechanistic reviews (Wackerhage 2019, Roberts 2023) and by trials where light and heavy loads taken to failure built similar muscle (Mitchell 2012, Morton 2016). The **"effective reps"** idea, that the last ~5 slow reps near failure do most of the stimulating, is a **theoretical model**, and the ~5 figure is an estimate from that model, not a count measured in any cited study. It's built from motor-unit recruitment and force-velocity physiology and has not been tested directly as a rep-counting rule. Morton 2019 supports type II fiber activation near failure at any load, but it used glycogen depletion as an indirect marker. Velocity-loss trials (Pareja-Blanco 2017, 2020, 2026; the Jukic 2023 review) generally found more growth with more velocity loss, but they mostly tested squats or bench press in young men, and higher velocity loss also meant more total reps. Among trials that roughly matched total volume, two small ones found no difference (Andersen 2024, Terada 2022) and one in strength-trained lifters found slightly more vastus lateralis growth with higher velocity loss (Myrholt 2023, n=16, 6 wk; whole-body lean mass (DXA) did not differ). EMG studies find heavier loads keep higher surface EMG even at failure (Jenkins 2015; Colosio 2026), so equal recruitment at light loads is inferred from growth trials, not shown directly.

Velocity-loss studies are context only; Carlsen has no velocity hardware. ## 2. Proximity to failure and reps in reserve (RIR)

**One-line answer:** Take most sets close to failure (about 0–2 reps left); stopping a rep or two short has built about as much muscle as true failure in most trials.

**Evidence grade:** Moderate — several meta-analyses and within-person trials in trained lifters agree, but the RIR dose-response rests on estimated RIR values and most trials are small (n≈14–42).

**(a) App justification:** Muscle growth tends to improve the closer each set gets to failure. A 2024 analysis of the research found hypertrophy kept rising as reps in reserve went down, while strength gains were about the same across a wide range of RIR. Going all the way to true failure on every set doesn't clearly beat stopping a rep or two short, and it costs more fatigue. In head-to-head trials in trained lifters, stopping 1–3 reps short of failure built about as much muscle as going to failure; when you do only one set of an exercise, taking it closer to failure may add a little.

**(b) Recommendation:** End most sets at 0–2 RIR (0–1 on isolation and machine work, 1–3 on heavy compound lifts), and save true failure for the last set of safe exercises.

Robinson 2024 found a clear dose-response, but its RIR values were *estimated* from study descriptions and the authors call the analysis exploratory. Newer within-person trials in trained lifters (Refalo 2024; Vasconcelos 2026 arms and legs) found 1–3 RIR about equal to failure, but each had about 18–19 people and 8 weeks. Lasevicius 2022 suggests failure matters more with light loads, since a light set stopped early leaves many reps (and much tension) on the table. Some results lean toward failure (Grgic 2022's trained subgroup; Hermann 2025 with one set per exercise; Martorelli 2017, where thickness rose significantly with failure and with volume-matched non-failure but not with lower-volume non-failure). Across 13 studies failure was favoured overall, but not when volume was equalized (Vieira 2021). This fits the idea that closeness to failure matters most when sets are few or loads light. People underestimate how many reps they have left by about 1 rep on average (Halperin 2022), so a "2 RIR" set may really be about 3 RIR. The exact RIR targets per exercise type in the recommendation are a Carlsen product rule built on this research, not a study finding.

## 3. Load and rep range (≈5–30 reps)

**One-line answer:** Roughly 5 to 30 reps per set build similar muscle when sets end close to or at failure; heavier loads build more max strength.

**Evidence grade:** Strong — several meta-analyses and trials in both new and trained lifters agree on similar growth across loads near failure and more strength with heavy loads; very light loads (<~30% 1RM) are less certain.

**(a) App justification:** Heavy and light weights build similar amounts of muscle, as long as sets are taken close to failure. That works across roughly 5 to 30 reps, in trials with both new and experienced lifters and in a meta-analysis that matched the total weight lifted. Heavier loads build more max strength. Very light, high-rep sets are more uncomfortable and fatiguing, and very heavy sets put more stress on joints. Most people do best in the middle of the range.

**(b) Recommendation:** Default to 6–12 reps on compound lifts and 10–20 on isolation exercises. Go heavier (3–6) if max strength is a goal, and use up to ~30 reps when joints need a break.

## 4. Training frequency per muscle and split choice

**One-line answer:** When weekly hard sets are equal, training a muscle once, twice or three times a week builds similar muscle; about twice a week is a practical default, not a requirement.

**Evidence grade:** Strong — several meta-analyses and many trials in trained and untrained adults find little effect of frequency once weekly volume is matched; the split-by-days pairings are a scheduling rule, not a study finding.

**(a) App justification:** If total weekly hard sets are the same, training a muscle once, twice, or three times a week builds similar muscle. Full-body and split routines produced similar growth in a 2024 analysis of 14 studies. Training a muscle about twice a week is a practical way to spread sets so sessions stay manageable. That's why full-body, upper/lower, and push/pull/legs splits are all good choices. A once-a-week "bro split" works too, but it crams a lot of sets into one session.

**(b) Recommendation:** Hit each muscle about 2× per week. Pick full body for 2–3 days a week, upper/lower for 4, and PPL for 5–6. A 5-day upper/lower + PPL hybrid (Upper, Lower, Push, Pull, Legs) also trains each muscle about twice a week. Only choose a bro split if you prefer it and keep the sets per session reasonable.

## 5. Volume: hard sets per session and diminishing returns

**One-line answer:** More hard sets generally build more muscle, with each extra set adding less; the per-session set numbers are a practical guide, not a tested limit.

**Evidence grade:** Strong for more sets building more muscle with diminishing returns (several meta-analyses); Limited for any per-session number, which is a product heuristic.

**(a) App justification:** Sets stopped far from failure stimulate less growth, so Carlsen only counts sets within about 0–3 reps in reserve (RIR) as hard sets. That's a counting convention, not a proven cutoff. More hard sets generally mean more growth, but each extra set adds less than the one before. In an early analysis, doing multiple sets per exercise was linked to about 40% larger growth effects than single sets. Spreading sets across sessions keeps each session shorter and fresher, which is practical advice: growth is similar either way when weekly hard sets are equal.

**(b) Recommendation:** Do about 2–4 hard sets per exercise and roughly 3–8 hard sets per muscle per session. Add sets only while performance holds and recovery is good. Splitting volume across days is a practical choice, not a growth requirement.

## 6. Rest intervals

**One-line answer:** Rest about 90 seconds to 3 minutes between sets; longer rest can make it easier to keep your reps and weight up, and both ends of that range have built similar muscle.

**Evidence grade:** Limited — a 9-study analysis found a small benefit for resting more than about a minute and no clear extra growth beyond about 90 seconds, but studies vary widely and none compared about 90 seconds directly with 2–3 minutes.

**(a) App justification:** Resting longer between sets lets you lift more weight for more reps on the next set, which likely means more tension per set. Research suggests a small, uncertain growth advantage for resting more than about a minute, probably because very short rest cuts the weight and reps you can manage. Beyond about 90 seconds, studies have not found clear extra growth, so about 90 seconds and 2–3 minutes are both reasonable. Longer rest may help more if max strength is a goal.

**(b) Recommendation:** Rest 90 seconds to 3 minutes between sets and pick what suits you. About 90 seconds keeps sessions shorter; 2–3 minutes can make it easier to match your previous set's weight and reps. Both work for building muscle.

## 7. Long muscle lengths, stretch and lengthened partials

**One-line answer:** Loading a muscle hard in its stretched position has built more muscle for some muscles, such as the hamstrings, triceps and calves; partial reps in the stretched range have grown muscle about as well as full reps, but not better.

**Evidence grade:** Moderate — several within-person trials show more growth when some muscles (hamstrings, triceps, calves) are trained at long lengths, but most are small and short; for lengthened partials, larger trials and trials in trained lifters found no advantage over full range of motion.

**(a) App justification:** Exercises that load a muscle hard while it's stretched tend to build more muscle than versions that load it mostly when it's short. For example, seated leg curls built more hamstring muscle than lying leg curls, overhead triceps extensions built more triceps than pushdown-style one-arm cable extensions with the upper arm at the side, and standing calf raises built more calf muscle than seated ones. Partial reps in the stretched part of the range grew muscle about as well as full reps; the largest trial (297 people) and several trials in experienced lifters found no meaningful difference, so full reps remain the default.

**(b) Recommendation:** Pick exercises that challenge the muscle in its stretched position, use full range of motion with control at the bottom, and optionally add lengthened partials at the end of a set once full reps fail (tested so far in two small calf studies in beginners, with mixed results).

## 8. Exercise selection: resistance profile and stability

**One-line answer:** Pick exercises you can load hard and progress, preferring stable ones that challenge the stretched position; free weights, machines and cables built similar muscle in head-to-head trials.

**Evidence grade:** Limited — equipment comparisons are consistent but small; the preference for stable exercises rests on short force tests and practical reasoning, and the one direct training trial found similar muscle growth at every stability level.

**(a) App justification:** Prefer exercises that let you push close to failure safely; for some muscles, such as the hamstrings and triceps, versions that challenge the stretched position grew more, while for the glutes, side delts and quads on the leg press, trials found no difference. Head-to-head trials often show similar growth (free weights vs machines, dumbbells vs cables), so pick what you can progress consistently. Different exercises for the same muscle can grow different regions: leg extensions grew the rectus femoris more than leg presses, while total quadriceps growth was similar. Planned variation and keeping the same exercises built similar overall muscle in recent trials, and rotating exercises at random every session added no growth. Stable exercises make heavy loading easier: in short tests on a Swiss ball, a very unstable surface, maximal force was about 20–70% lower than on a bench or chair. Less stable versions still build muscle; in a 7-week trial, squats on a Smith machine, with free weights or on a wobble board gave similar muscle growth.

**(b) Recommendation:** For each muscle, pick 1–2 exercises you can progress consistently (all else equal, prefer stable ones that challenge the stretched position), keep them for a training block, and add planned variation rather than swapping every week. Less stable variations also build muscle and are fine to keep in a programme.

## 9. Progressive overload and auto-regulation (next-session load)

**One-line answer:** Add reps until you hit the top of the range, then add weight; how hard the last sets felt decides the next jump.

**Evidence grade:** Moderate — progressing reps or load both worked in two trials, and two 8-week trials found that adding load beat repeating it, but only in untrained participants (women, single-joint arm exercises); autoregulation evidence is mostly about strength, and the exact progression rule is a product convention.

**(a) App justification:** Muscle keeps growing when the training challenge keeps rising. In trained lifters, adding reps at the same weight and adding weight at the same reps built similar muscle. In untrained women, raising the load whenever the top of an 8–12 rep range was reached roughly doubled arm-muscle growth over 8 weeks compared with repeating the same load. Setting the load from reps in reserve produced muscle gains similar to a fixed percentage plan and lets the weight move with good and bad days.

**(b) Recommendation:** Use double progression: build reps to the top of the range at the target RIR, then add the smallest available weight step and restart at the bottom of the range; hold the load when sets end harder than the target RIR.

## 10. Weekly hard sets per muscle (direct and indirect sets)

**One-line answer:** Count a hard set as 1 for the muscle it targets and ½ for helper muscles; more sets help, but each extra set adds a bit less.

**Evidence grade:** Moderate — several meta-analyses agree that more weekly sets give more growth with diminishing returns, but trials in trained lifters are mixed and the ½ weighting for helper muscles is a statistical best fit, not a measured value.

**(a) App justification:** More weekly hard sets per muscle generally produce more growth, with smaller gains from each additional set. Compound lifts also train helper muscles, and a 2026 meta-regression of 67 studies fit the data best when those indirect sets counted as half a set. Some muscles get little from compound lifts: squats grew the quadriceps but not the rectus femoris or hamstrings in one trial in untrained men, and leg extensions grew the rectus femoris far more than squats in another in untrained women.

**(b) Recommendation:** Track weekly hard sets per muscle as direct sets × 1 plus indirect sets × ½, start from the weekly sets you already do and add sets gradually only while performance keeps improving, and add a direct exercise for muscles that compound lifts train poorly (rectus femoris, hamstrings).

## 11. Exercise substitution (add, remove, swap)

**One-line answer:** Swap to an exercise that trains the same muscle through a similar stretch, then stick with it long enough to progress.

**Evidence grade:** Moderate — equipment comparisons (two meta-analyses plus several trials) are consistent, but regional and stretch effects come from small, short trials, often in untrained people, and the swap rules are reasoning from those trials rather than tested.

**(a) App justification:** Two exercises for the same muscle usually build similar overall size; free weights vs machines, dumbbell vs cable lateral raises and different cable-curl positions all produced similar growth. Swaps matter more when they change which region of a muscle is loaded or how stretched it gets: overhead triceps extensions grew the triceps about 1.4 times as much as arms-at-side extensions, and leg extensions grew the rectus femoris more than squats. Mixing a few exercises for a muscle helped some regions grow in some trials but not in a 2025 trial in 70 women, while rotating exercises at random every session did not add growth, though it improved motivation.

**(b) Recommendation:** When swapping, keep the same target muscle, a similar stretched-position challenge and a similar rep range, and keep core exercises in place for at least a training block so progress can be tracked.

## 12. Per-session limits (sets per muscle per session)

**One-line answer:** No study has pinned down a per-session set limit; splitting a large weekly total across two or more sessions is a cautious default.

**Evidence grade:** Limited — few small trials compare per-session volume directly, most compare whole programmes, and no trustworthy study defines a per-session ceiling.

**(a) App justification:** When weekly sets were matched, doing them in one session or spreading them over several produced broadly similar growth in most trials, and one trial comparing five full-body sessions a week with a once-a-week split favoured the frequent sessions. Doubling a single session from 5 to 10 sets per exercise gave no extra growth, and in one trial slightly less trunk lean mass. A session of 21 lower-body sets felt harder and slowed perceived recovery compared with 7 or 14 sets; long-term effects of such sessions are untested.

**(b) Recommendation:** Show a soft warning, not a hard cap, when one session holds a large share of a muscle's weekly sets, and suggest moving some sets to another day.

## 13. Onboarding: experience, session length and equipment

### 13a. Experience level

**One-line answer:** Beginners grow fastest on modest training; experienced lifters progress more slowly and need more precise effort and progression.

**Evidence grade:** Moderate — slower gains in trained lifters are consistent across trials and meta-analyses, but the key direct trial is small (n=16) and starting-volume rules by experience have not been tested.

**(a) App justification:** People new to lifting gain size and strength faster than experienced lifters on the same programme. In a 21-week trial, untrained men grew their quadriceps by 5.6% while strength athletes showed no gain. Untrained people respond strongly to almost any programme; setting starting volume by experience is a programming convention rather than a tested rule.

**(b) Recommendation:** Start beginners at the lower end of weekly hard sets and progress mainly through reps and load; start experienced users at higher weekly sets with closer RIR targets and slower expected progress.

### 13b. Session length

**One-line answer:** Keep the hard sets and normal rests; cut long warm-ups and extra exercises first, or pair non-competing exercises.

**Evidence grade:** Moderate — strength gains with minimal time are supported by several trials, but hypertrophy is less certain because more volume tended to build more muscle, and superset trials are mostly short-term.

**(a) App justification:** Short sessions can work if they keep the hard sets: trained men gained strength with just three 13-minute sessions a week, although longer, higher-volume sessions built more muscle. Pairing opposing exercises or using supersets fit more work into less time in short-term studies, and a 2025 meta-analysis found supersets built similar muscle and strength to traditional sets, based on only two long-term studies.

**(b) Recommendation:** For short sessions, prioritise multi-joint exercises, pair non-competing exercises as supersets or use drop sets on isolation exercises, and keep warm-ups to exercise-specific ramp-up sets; use longer sessions to add sets for priority muscles.

### 13c. Equipment

**One-line answer:** Free weights and machines build similar muscle; push-ups matched a light (40% 1RM) bench press in one small trial.

**Evidence grade:** Moderate — free weights vs machines and light vs heavy loads near failure are well supported, but evidence for bands and bodyweight is thin (strength-only, or one small push-up trial).

**(a) App justification:** Free weights and machines produced similar muscle growth in two meta-analyses, and push-ups matched a light (40% 1RM) bench press for chest and triceps growth in young men. Elastic bands matched conventional weights for strength gains, though direct muscle-growth comparisons for bands are scarce. Light loads can build similar muscle when taken close to failure, but one trial found a very light 20% 1RM load grew less than heavier loads with volume load matched (a meta-analysis of volume-matched studies found no clear difference), so minimal-equipment plans need sets that stay challenging.

**(b) Recommendation:** Build the plan from the user's available equipment, and when load is light use higher reps taken close to failure or harder bodyweight variations to keep sets challenging.

## 14. Rep-range and RIR accuracy by experience level

**One-line answer:** Most lifters misjudge reps left by about one to two, usually underestimating, and guesses get better near failure.

**Evidence grade:** Moderate — a meta-analysis and several newer trials agree that RIR estimates are imperfect and better near failure, but most studies are single sessions with small samples and results vary widely.

**(a) App justification:** Across 12 studies (414 participants), people underestimated how many reps they had left by about one rep on average, with large differences between studies. Estimates were slightly, not clearly, more accurate close to failure, more accurate in sets of 12 reps or fewer, and only trivially better in later sets. Training experience made little difference in that analysis and in several newer trials, although one large study found a tendency toward better accuracy with more experience. Practice seems to help: in two newer trials, estimates improved over several weeks of repeated sessions. Resistance-trained lifters predicting 1 and 3 reps in reserve on the bench press were off by about 0.65 reps on average.

**(b) Recommendation:** Treat logged RIR as an estimate within about one to two reps, use RIR ranges rather than single numbers, and give the last sets of an exercise the most weight when adjusting load.

## 15. Deloads and fatigue management

**One-line answer:** Deload weeks are optional: in the few trials, planned breaks neither added nor cost muscle, so a well-designed split doesn't strictly need one; about every 8 weeks is a sensible default.

**Evidence grade:** Limited — only one controlled deload trial in trained lifters (a full week off, not a reduced week), the other trials are in untrained people, and no trial has tested deload timing; the ~8-week suggestion is a Carlsen product choice, while coach and athlete surveys report deloads about every 4–6 weeks (Bell 2022) or 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks (Rogerson 2024).

**(a) App justification:** In trained lifters, a one-week break midway through a 9-week programme led to similar muscle growth but smaller strength gains than training straight through. In untrained men, lighter weeks at weeks 4 and 8, or 3-week breaks between 6-week blocks, gave similar overall growth to continuous training. Coaches and athletes widely use deloads, typically about a week every 4–6 weeks with fewer sets and easier effort, but that practice rests mainly on expert consensus and surveys rather than trials. Carlsen suggests about every 8 weeks, a little less often than the surveys report; this interval is a product choice, since trials have not tested deload timing and so far show no muscle benefit or cost from planned breaks.

**(b) Recommendation:** Deloads are optional. Carlsen suggests a deload week (fewer sets, more reps in reserve, same exercises) about every 8 weeks, or sooner if performance stalls across several sessions.
